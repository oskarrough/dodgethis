# World

Read this before you draw, write, compose or name anything. It sets the mood; [look.md](look.md) holds the art rules, [sound.md](sound.md) the audio rules, and the Voice section of [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md#voice) says how we write. Where this page and those disagree, they win.

The name and the setting are still open in issue #25, and the game is called Dodgethis until Oskar says otherwise. What follows is the current direction. **Settled** means it is built or Oskar has said so; **Open** means nobody should treat it as decided, least of all in a commit.

## The setting

High over a sea of cloud hang the mesas: flat-topped towers of red and violet stone, cut loose from whatever ground once held them and kept aloft by an old magic that nobody tends and nobody quite trusts. Their tops are courtyards. Someone laid them with terracotta flags long ago, built stairs that go nowhere and arches that open onto sky, and then left. Moebius would know the light, a huge pale sun over an empty desert of air, and Escher would know the stairs.

People come up to fight on these courts the way a village gathers for a wrestling match: for the sport of it, with no war behind it. The lobby is a terrero, the sand ring of Canarian wrestling, here paved in sun-bleached terracotta and ringed by oxide-red stands and a basalt wall, under a pastel sky that goes from apricot at the horizon to violet overhead, with other mesas drifting in the distance. A stone portal stands at one side. When enough heroes walk into it, its mouth fills with light and carries them down to the court below.

The desert mesa is the hub, and every court is its own island floating in the same sky, with its own country on top. Overthrow is a green isle by day, pale stone with moss on its lip and white water pouring off its edges into cloud. Flagfall is a night jungle, where the gardens on the lower rims wake and their orchid bells glow. What holds them together is the cloud sea under all of them, the one navy line and the same type. Red and blue are banners and sashes picked on the day, not nations, so a mirror match needs no excuse.

Every few minutes the Ball falls out of the sky, a shard of something bright. You see its shadow first, which is fair warning, and it lands hot and hits harder than anything made up here until it cools. The keeps are faceted crystals on stepped plinths, guarded by stone towers, and the minions are small nameless things of the mesas that follow the nearest keep and walk at whatever it points at.

The tallest of them is Altamesa, and it is hollow. Mesa is Spanish for table, so the name is a high table, which is roughly what it looks like from a passing cloud. Inside it waits a dungeon for a party of three, kept by seven characters who have had a long time alone to grow strange: Manos at the gate, Tinaja and Botijo in the court, Carillon and Espejo down the sun wing, Fornax and Barquero down the shade wing, and Gnomon at the bottom, who has kept time over the mesa longer than anyone has kept count ([deck](https://claude.ai/artifact/GRXdewN9rWPFrdpSKuD8H7)). Their cast list is below.

## The feeling

It should feel like a Saturday afternoon on a rooftop that happens to be a mile up: warm, a little dangerous, nobody in a hurry until the Ball drops. The charm is real but it has calluses. Creatures are small and funny and made of hide, bark, stone, bone and enamel, worn at the edges, with small eyes under a brow and at least one hard angle. If a frame would pass as a plush-toy advert, it has gone too cute; if it would pass as a grimdark box cover, it has gone too far the other way. The play itself feels like Melee: instant, snappy, never waiting on an animation.

The world shows rather than tells. A countdown is a portal filling with light, a waiting hero is a glowing seat, a spawn is a slow ring on the floor. Words are for when nothing else will do, and then they are set like a 1990s Japanese game manual on a Swiss grid, flush left, with big index numerals.

The humour is dry and generous, the kind that trusts you to get it. Super Mario Odyssey is the model for warmth: its desert town is Tostarena, a sun-toasted sand joke that works in half the languages on earth, and nobody stops to explain it. Smash Bros. is the model for confidence: a stage is Corneria or Onett, a proper name said once and then simply true. We never wink at the player, never write a pun that only English gets, and never let a creature be silly for its own sake. A thing is funny because of what it does: a kiln that grows hotter the longer you dawdle is funnier than a kiln called something funny.

When the text gets long, count the articles. A paragraph that reads "the Gate, the Court, the Kiln, the Ferry" has turned into an inventory; give the place or the creature its name and let the sentence move.

## Names

Names are characters first. A boss, a place or a hero should sound like someone you could meet, and a child in Madrid, Osaka or Lagos should be able to shout it across a playground after hearing it once.

- **A proper name, no article.** Altamesa, not the Hollow Mesa; Gnomon, not the Sundial. Common things that have no personality stay common nouns (the Ball, the portal, a waystone, a keep), and that is fine, but anything you fight or visit gets a name.
- **Short and sayable.** Two or three syllables, at most four. Spelled the way it sounds in most European languages and in Japanese katakana, with no silent letters and no apostrophes.
- **No "X of Y", no compounds of two English words.** "Gate of Hands", "Kiln Gallery" and "Shadowbane" are descriptions, not names. Descriptions belong in the sentence after the name.
- **Rooted, not invented.** Take a real word from a language that suits the place, ideally one whose meaning is the joke or the clue (a gnomon is the pin that casts a sundial's shadow; a botijo is a clay jug you drink from by its spout). Invented fantasy syllables (Xal'thuun) are out, and so are brand-like coinages.
- **Not childish.** Petey, Big Bertha and Mr. Pots are the wrong side of charm. The name should still sound right if the creature turns out to be very old.
- **One initial per cast.** Within a dungeon or a map, no two characters share a first letter, so callouts in a fight never collide.
- **Check it abroad.** Before a name ships, look up what it means in Spanish, Portuguese, French, German, Japanese and Turkish, and drop it if it is rude or silly in any of them.

Code follows the player's name once it is settled: `encounters/gnomon/`, not `encounters/sundial/`. Until then the folder keeps its old name, so a rename is one decision and one commit.

### Where names come from

There is no house language. A name comes from wherever the best word for that character lives, as long as it passes the rules above: Gnomon is Greek, Carillon is French and Dutch, Salvia is Latin, and none of them is there to make a point about geography. Overthrow and Flagfall are courts in one world, not countries, so no map gets a language of its own.

The one exception is Altamesa. The dungeon has a Spanish and Canarian flavour, because it sits under the terrero and the lucha canaria, so its keepers and its lesser things lean that way: Altamesa, Tinaja, Botijo, Espejo, Barquero, Manos, Tagoror, the Tiestos and the boons. That flavour belongs to the dungeon. Heroes come from all over and should not sound as if they all grew up in the same village.

Canarian words come from the islands' first people, the Guanches, and a few have survived in place names and wrestling (a _tagoror_ was the stone circle where they met in council). Use them with care and only where the meaning fits; they are heritage first.

## The cast of Altamesa

Each line is the character in one breath, for whoever writes its fight, draws it or names its attacks. The folder names in brackets are the code as it stands today.

- **Manos** (`manos`). A wall of red clay hands at the mouth of the mesa that grabs whoever stands still and holds on for ten seconds. Not cruel, only very bored, and glad of the company.
- **Tinaja and Botijo** (`tinajaBotijo`). Two jars who have kept the central court together for longer than either will admit. Tinaja is fat and slow and sits where she likes; Botijo is thin and quick and spits cold water from his spout. Break one and the other takes it personally.
- **Tagoror**. Not a boss: the central court itself, a ring of standing stones where the twins hold council and both wings begin.
- **Carillon** (`carillon`). A choir of hanging bells in the sun wing that rings in rounds. Each note lands where the last one pointed, so the fight is a song you learn by being hit by it.
- **Espejo** (`espejo`). A tall silvered pane at the end of the sun wing that fights you with your own hero, a beat late. It has never had an original idea and is proud of it.
- **Fornax** (`fornax`). A great kiln in the shade wing with a door like a mouth. It is patient, it grows hotter the longer you stay, and everything it has fired is still in there with you.
- **Barquero** (`barquero`). A ferryman who poles a stone boat across the cloud at the bottom of the shade wing. He takes a toll, he does not hurry, and he will happily push you off.
- **Gnomon** (`gnomon`). The pin of the great sundial at the bottom of the stair. Its shadow sweeps the floor, and the shadow is the attack, as the Ball's shadow is the warning. It has kept time over the mesa since before the first flagstone, and it would like you to know you are late.

Lesser things keep plain common nouns from the same palette, with no article. Manos's throwers are the **Tiestos**, the broken shards of old pots. The boons a wing boss leaves behind are **Lupa**, a cracked magnifying glass; **Azogue**, the quicksilver that backs an old mirror; **Bizcocho**, the potter's word for clay fired once and not yet glazed (it is also a sponge cake, and nobody on the mesa finds this as funny as they used to); and **Tara**, a small clay idol after the one the Guanches left on Gran Canaria.

## Prompting images

Paste the style block from [look.md](look.md#concept-art) whole, follow its recipe, then add the nouns for the scene from Keywords above. For the front end (splash, lobby, cards) add: "ligne claire, thin navy line, pastels mixed toward cream, apricot horizon to violet sky, floating red mesas in haze, terracotta courtyard". For a map card add: "Vallotton woodcut, a handful of flat colours, ink masses, gouged sky".

Avoid: plush, felt, glossy eyes, blush, chibi faces, mobile-card-game rendering, Hearthstone, black outlines, rainbow or neon palettes, gold outside the Ball, schoolyard or seaside props, sci-fi technology, photoreal surfaces, busy texture on the floor, and the words "cute", "fun" and "toy-like", which drag the model straight to Hearthstone.

## Prompting music and sound

Music is made in Oskar's own tool, [Jukebox](https://jukebox.0sk.ar) (the `oskarrough/jukebox` repo), so Dodgethis never builds a music tool of its own.

Start from the menu prompt in [sound.md](sound.md#splash-and-lobby-prompt) and keep its scene tempos (lobby about 112, match 120, clutch 138). Keywords: Azymuth jazz-funk warmth and Yellow Magic Orchestra machine, Fender Rhodes with 9ths and 11ths, singing fretless-style bass, light samba kit with shaker and cuíca, glassy Minimoog portamento, square-wave arpeggios, vocoder pads, gentle tape saturation, wide stereo, sunset over floating mesas, stretching before a match, seamless loop, no vocals. Effects take one material family per source (Fletcher string and wood, Mitts leather and thump, stone and iron for structures, bright glass and a low bloom for the Ball, quiet wood and paper for menus), land on the frame and keep fight tails under 400 ms.

Avoid: kazoo, cartoon boings, plush squeaks, beeps in menus, orchestral fantasy bombast, dread or horror drones, EDM drops, vocals, and anything that sits over the effects in a fight.
